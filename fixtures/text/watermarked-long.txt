When did the library start considering books "in context?" "The library was made aware of some of the material we have on display to date as a result of a series of letters circulated as a part of the National Library Week consultation process," the policy states. "The purpose of the letter was to provide the public with feedback from other members of the public."

"It also came across as the library taking an extreme 'you are on display forever' approach to collection development, and that seemed wrong to me," she said.

The letter came from one John White, a former executive officer on the committee for the British Humanist Association. In it, he wrote that "recent research has focused on our tendency as a society to focus on the destruction of things," and that the British library's books should instead be seen as a "collection of stories, or pieces of history, or stories that hold important stories to tell today."

And that's when the policy first shifted a bit. It now says that the library's collection "belongs to everyone, and we should strive to do what we can to make them more accessible" by allowing for online access.

"I think it's wonderful to have an open and inclusive culture — and I certainly don't want us to turn our backs on diversity," the library's chief curator, Deborah Davis, told the Washington Post. "But I think there's also this sense among library users that we're not doing enough, or that there are some things we want to avoid that have nothing to do with books."

The change may be a way to address that, although Davis suggested that it shouldn't be considered a formal policy directive. A year ago, the library's current curator, Jennifer Caffalini, told The New York Times that she wanted to "look at this from an individual perspective."

"So we really wanted to put those feelings about why we do some things differently, but also think it's in the public good if we do it and not just out of our personal interest, to bring our collection in from the cold," Davis said.

This kind of personal perspective doesn't just apply to books; the library's book section also contains several books that are about art and cultural criticism that don't conform to the mainstream model of historical fiction or nonfiction. (According to the library's new standards, the books must be novellas or short nonfiction essays.) The Library of Congress does not require libraries to stock a selection of such books — in part, because nonfiction traditionally doesn't sell as well as historical fiction, an editor at the American Association of Publishers told the Times in November — but the library's current policy indicates that it would want to feature such material if it did.

According to the AP, Caffalini has said that she would like to see that feature expand. For now, though, the library can do exactly what it wants with historical fiction. Though it does consider them in the "context of the entire collection," there are no plans at the moment to create a specialized section.

Though the library is still working out the details of its new policy, what comes from the library is a shift of how the collection, at least in part, is approached. For now, "most libraries in the U.S. do not own historical fiction," the AP reported. That's partly because historically-themed fiction has not been well received among librarians, who see it as a less scholarly way of telling a story and an inappropriate focus for an otherwise literary collection — one that is often described as a collection for children "with a history degree."

More importantly, though, historical fiction is often difficult for libraries to acquire in part because of the difficulty of finding original hardcovers in good condition. As the Los Angeles Times noted, "For more than 80 years, libraries have turned to hardcovers, not paper, for their acquisitions of rare books." But for the last four decades or so, a growing portion of librarians have come to consider paper books as a bad investment.

"I think it's a very good bet to say that if library users, and library donors, were in charge of library policy," Davis told the Washington Post, "they would take a very dim view of the large amount of paper books that we currently own," especially for the "many, many people who now use the library every day and have a much fuller and richer world inside with their books than they have had in previous generations."
